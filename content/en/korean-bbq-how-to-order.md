Korean BBQ (gogi-gui, 고기구이) is one of the meals visitors most want to try in Korea, and it is easier than it looks once you know how the menu works. You order raw meat by the serving, it is grilled at your table, and the side dishes come free.

> **Quick answer:** Sit down, order meat **by the serving (인분, *inbun*)**, usually **at least two servings** to start, and add a drink. Side dishes (banchan), lettuce and dipping sauces are included and refilled at no charge. In Seoul, one 200 g serving of pork belly (samgyeopsal) averaged about **₩21,000** in the Korea Consumer Agency's 2026 price survey. Staff often grill the meat for you; if not, cook it yourself and cut it with the scissors provided. You pay at the counter on the way out.

## How ordering at a Korean BBQ restaurant works

1. **Take a seat.** At most casual places you just walk in and sit at any free table. Busy spots may have a waiting list on a tablet by the door.
2. **Order the meat.** Prices on the menu are per serving (1인분). A serving is usually 150–200 g of raw meat. Many restaurants ask for **a minimum of two servings** for the first order, even for one diner, and some simply will not seat solo guests at peak times.
3. **Add drinks and extras.** Soju, beer, soft drinks, rice (공기밥, *gonggibap*) and stews such as doenjang-jjigae are ordered separately.
4. **Grill and eat.** Staff light the grill, bring the meat and banchan, and at many places come back to turn and cut it.
5. **Order more if you like.** Extra servings can usually be ordered one at a time.
6. **Pay at the front.** There is normally no bill brought to the table. Take your receipt or table number to the counter. Tipping is not expected in Korea.

More restaurants now use **table-top tablets** for ordering, which often have an English option. If there is a call button on the table, press it to get staff's attention; saying *"Jeogiyo!"* (저기요, "excuse me") also works.

## What to order: common cuts and their Korean names

| Korean | Romanized | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| 삼겹살 | samgyeopsal | Pork belly, the classic and usually the cheapest choice |
| 목살 | moksal | Pork neck/shoulder, leaner and chewier than belly |
| 항정살 | hangjeongsal | Pork jowl, small fatty pieces with a crunchy bite |
| 갈매기살 | galmaegisal | Pork skirt (diaphragm), lean and beefy |
| 돼지갈비 | dwaeji-galbi | Marinated pork ribs, sweet soy marinade |
| 소갈비 / 생갈비 | so-galbi / saeng-galbi | Beef short ribs, marinated / unmarinated, more expensive |
| 등심 | deungsim | Beef sirloin |
| 차돌박이 | chadolbagi | Thinly sliced beef brisket that cooks in seconds |
| 우삼겹 | usamgyeop | Thin-sliced beef short plate |

A safe first order for two people is two servings of samgyeopsal, then one serving of moksal or dwaeji-galbi if you are still hungry. Marinated meat burns faster, so many places grill it after the plain cuts or change the grill plate for it.

Restaurants usually specialize: a **pork BBQ** place (돼지고기 전문) is far cheaper than a **hanwoo** (Korean native beef) restaurant, where a single serving can cost several times as much. Check prices on the menu by the entrance before you sit down.

## How much does Korean BBQ cost in Seoul?

Prices vary by neighborhood and quality, so treat these as a guide, as of November 2026:

| Item | Typical price |
|---|---|
| Pork belly (samgyeopsal), 200 g serving | about ₩21,000 Seoul average (Korea Consumer Agency, 2026) |
| Bibimbap (for comparison) | about ₩11,600 Seoul average (Feb 2026) |
| Rice, stew, drinks | ordered and charged separately |
| Hanwoo beef | much higher; check the menu |

The official averages come from the Korea Consumer Agency's **Chamgagyeok (참가격)** price portal, which tracks dining-out prices for eight common dishes each month. The pork belly average rose about 4% year on year in 2026. Popular restaurants in Myeongdong, Gangnam and Seongsu, and premium aged or heritage-breed pork, often cost more than the average.

To budget, multiply the per-serving price by the number of servings: at the Seoul average, three servings of pork belly for two people come to roughly ₩64,000 before rice, stews and drinks are added. Cards are accepted almost everywhere; see our [guide to paying in Korea](../paying-in-korea-cards-cash-wowpass/).

## What comes free with the meal

These are normally included and refilled on request (sometimes from a self-service bar):

- **Banchan:** small side dishes such as kimchi, pickled radish, bean sprouts and salads.
- **Ssam vegetables:** lettuce and perilla leaves for wrapping the meat.
- **Sauces:** ssamjang (a thick bean and chili paste), sesame oil with salt and pepper, and sometimes a soy-based dip.
- **Garlic and green chili**, which many people grill alongside the meat.
- **Water**, usually self-service from a dispenser or a bottle on the table.

Some places also add a free steamed egg (gyeran-jjim) or soybean stew; others charge for them. If something is unclear, ask *"Seobiseu-yeyo?"* (서비스예요?, "is this complimentary?").

## How to eat Korean BBQ

- **Make a ssam (wrap):** take a lettuce leaf, add a piece of meat, a dab of ssamjang, a slice of grilled garlic and maybe some kimchi, then eat it in one bite.
- **Cut with the scissors.** Kitchen scissors (gawi) and tongs are standard; use them to cut the meat into bite-size pieces on the grill.
- **Don't overcook pork belly.** Turn it once the bottom is golden; staff may do this for you.
- **Finish with carbs.** A common ending is cold noodles (naengmyeon), a bowl of rice or **fried rice made on the grill** (볶음밥, *bokkeumbap*) with the leftover kimchi.

## Table manners to know

- **Pouring drinks:** in Korea people pour for each other rather than themselves. When an older person pours for you, hold your glass with two hands.
- **Turn slightly away** when drinking in front of someone older; this is a polite gesture, not a strict rule for visitors.
- **Let staff change the grill.** If the plate is blackened, ask *"Bulpan gara juseyo"* (불판 갈아 주세요, "please change the grill plate"). It is free.
- **Clothes will smell of smoke.** Many restaurants provide plastic bags or covers for coats; use them.

Nobody expects visitors to know every custom. Being polite to staff and not leaving a pile of uneaten meat matters more.

## Eating Korean BBQ alone

Solo dining (honbap, 혼밥) is common in Korea, but BBQ is the hardest meal to do alone because of two-serving minimums. Options:

- Look for restaurants that state **1인분 주문 가능** ("single servings available") or have counter seats with individual grills.
- Go at **off-peak times** (around 15:00–17:00), when staff are more flexible.
- Try a **lunch set** at a BBQ restaurant, or a dish that is cooked in the kitchen, such as dwaeji-bulgogi (stir-fried spicy pork) with rice.

Map apps make this easier: search "1인 고기" or "혼밥 고기" in [Naver Map or Kakao Map](../google-maps-korea-naver-kakao-map/) to find places that welcome single diners.

## FAQ

**Do I have to order two servings if I am eating alone?**
At many BBQ restaurants, yes, the first order must be at least two servings. Some places offer single servings; look for "1인분 주문 가능" or ask before you sit down.

**Do I need to tip?**
No. Tipping is not part of Korean restaurant culture, and the price on the menu is what you pay.

**Are side dishes really free?**
Yes, banchan and lettuce are included and normally refilled at no extra cost. Rice, stews, noodles and drinks are usually charged.

**Will the staff cook the meat for me?**
Often, especially at busy restaurants in tourist areas, but not always. If no one comes back, it is fine to grill it yourself.

**Is Korean BBQ suitable for vegetarians?**
Not really; the meal is built around meat. Banchan, rice and some stews are meat-free, but stews may use meat or anchovy stock.

**Can I make a reservation?**
Most casual pork BBQ places are walk-in only. Popular or high-end beef restaurants may take bookings through Catch Table or Naver.
